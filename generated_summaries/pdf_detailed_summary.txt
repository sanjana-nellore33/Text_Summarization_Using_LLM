Text messaging, which allows users to send and receive typed messages quickly without phone conversations, has become a popular tool for teenagers and businesses. Along with this popularity came the creation of "textspeak"—the use of abbreviations, symbols, short phrases, and omitted punctuation. Written by Michaela Cullington, an essay originally published in *Young Scholars in Writing* explores the intense debate surrounding whether texting negatively harms or positively enhances student writing and communication skills.

Critics of texting argue that it ruins spelling, diminishes writing abilities, and hurts emotional expression. A 2008 *USA Today* article cited low writing proficiency among high school seniors, while former teacher Jacquie Ream and linguistics professor Naomi Baron both blame texting for declining student mechanics. Teachers report finding text abbreviations—such as "2," "gr8," "dat," and "wut"—along with missing punctuation in student assignments. Cullington’s interviewed teachers noted that texting reinforces simplistic writing and hinders critical thinking, though their claims rely on limited personal experiences. 

Conversely, supporters argue that texting benefits students by providing writing motivation, practice, confidence, and creativity. Linguist David Crystal, in *Txtng: The Gr8 Db8*, notes that texting encourages concise summarization, diplomacy, and extra literacy practice for struggling students. Other educators observe that texting helps teens develop an individual voice and enjoy formal writing by providing a comfortable, constant form of communication. Crystal and David Warlick view text abbreviations positively as a natural "language evolution."

To investigate this controversy, Cullington utilized three research methods: surveying seven diverse student friends, questioning two high school teachers, and analyzing twenty 8-to-10-page research papers from first-year college courses. The findings indicated that texting has a minimal effect on student writing. Specifically, the analysis of the student research papers revealed zero examples of textspeak. 

Expert and student habits strongly support these findings. University of Illinois professor Dennis Baron points out that students do not use textspeak in formal writing and usually abandon abbreviations by high school or college. Student surveys show that while youths start texting around the ninth grade and send 30 to 100 messages daily, they use abbreviations only occasionally and know they are unacceptable in academic work. Furthermore, a Midwestern University study of 86 students found that texting and abbreviations do not negatively impact spelling proficiency or test scores. Cullington, a frequent texter herself, confirms that she never uses abbreviations in formal writing and experiences no negative spelling impacts.

Ultimately, Cullington asserts that actual evidence outweighs anecdotal concerns, proving that texting does not interfere with standard written English or overall writing abilities. While critics point to declining writing standards and supporters argue texting prepares teens for formal writing, concrete research shows students easily differentiate between casual texting and formal writing contexts. Finally, comparing texts reveals that while Cullington focuses narrowly on how texting affects writing, author Sherry Turkle addresses how texting impacts broader human communication.
